1.1 · The reframe that does most of the work

Teachers treat a chatbot like a calculator — a private tool, sitting on the desk, doing a job and forgetting it. It is not that. Almost every consumer AI service is a commercial product whose business depends on the data flowing through it, and your conversation is retained in an account history, in logs, in backups, and in whatever a support engineer can see.

The working model

Treat an AI chatbot as a potentially public data warehouse, not a private tool.

You do not have to believe anything sinister about any particular company for this to hold. It follows from how the services are built and paid for. And it is the only assumption that produces the right behaviour under uncertainty — which matters, because the terms change faster than any school's policy review cycle.

1.2 · Why this is a gate and not a topic

Module 3 will put you in front of a real AI output and ask you to find everything wrong with it. You will find a fabricated source, a flattened task, a stereotype, a confident error. Those are the ordinary failure modes, and every one of them has the same remedy: you read it, you catch it, you reject it, and nothing has happened.

Privacy is the exception, and the difference is not one of degree.

Faults in the output

Bias · flatness · hallucination · confident error · plagiarism · lowered demand

Discovered by reading. Fixed by rejecting. The cost of being wrong is a few minutes and a better second draft. You get as many attempts as you like.

What you put in

A name · a photograph · an IEP · a behaviour note · a disclosure

Done at the moment you press enter. No review stage, no second draft, no undo. Deleting your own chat history destroys your record of it and removes nothing from theirs.

So the two belong in different categories. The rest of this course teaches you to be sceptical of what comes out. This module teaches you to be disciplined about what goes in, and discipline is the right word, because it has to hold on the afternoon when you are tired and the deadline is tomorrow.

1.3 · What counts as personal data

Broader than most teachers assume, and the categories people forget are the ones that matter most.

CategoryIncludes
Direct identifiersNames, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, student ID numbers, national ID numbers, dates of birth.
Educational recordsGrades, transcripts, reading ages, assessment data against a named child, disciplinary records, SEND status, IEPs and support plans.
Health informationMedical records, conditions, allergies, medication, therapy or counselling notes.
Financial informationFamily income, fee status, bursary or financial aid information, free-meal eligibility.
Behavioural recordsBehaviour reports, sanctions, pastoral and counselling notes, safeguarding concerns.
Images and recordingsPhotographs or video of students — including a photographed page of a book, because handwriting identifies a child and the image file carries metadata.
Communication logsMessages, emails and correspondence with students or parents.
CombinationsAnything that identifies one child when put together with what is reasonably available. “The Year 9 girl who joined in January and has an EHCP” contains no name and identifies one person.

The middle five categories are treated as more sensitive again under most data protection regimes. As a working rule for this course, they never go near an AI tool in any form, aggregated or not. The taxonomy here follows the categories commonly used in school data-protection guidance; Eric Curts's Control Alt Achieve sets out a similar list for US schools and is worth reading alongside your own school's policy.

What may go in, and it is enough for everything in this course

The task. The curriculum statement. The rubric. Your own planning. And anonymised aggregate patterns — “eleven of twenty-eight thought the heavier ball would land first.” A count over a class of reasonable size identifies nobody and is the single most useful sentence you can give a model.

1.6 · The case nobody plans for: the student discloses

Everything above is about what you paste. There is a second exposure, and it is created by task design rather than by carelessness — which is why it belongs in this course and not only in a safeguarding policy.

Set a reflective task. “Write about a time you overcame a challenge.” Allow or require an AI tool as part of it. A student then types, into a consumer service, under their own account:

Write a personal essay about overcoming a challenge. Focus on how I dealt with being bullied for two years by a girl in my form and her group. It got so bad I changed schools and started seeing a therapist for the depression and anxiety it caused. Then my parents separated.

That is a named peer, a mental health history, therapy, and family circumstances — several of the most sensitive categories in the table above — sent to a third party by a child, because of a task you set. No policy about what teachers may paste touches it.

What to do about it at design time
  • Assume the prompt is the invitation. If a task invites personal disclosure, it does not get an AI step. Reflective, pastoral and personal-narrative work stays off the tools.
  • Teach minimum necessary information before the first task that uses a tool, not after. Students need the same rule you have, in their words: share the least that makes it work, and never anyone else's name.
  • Offer the hypothetical. “Write about a character who faced this” gets the same skill practised without the disclosure. Where the personal version matters pedagogically, it is handwritten and it stays in the room.
  • Watch for the disclosure that arrives anyway. A student may tell you something in a task that no one planned. That goes to the designated safeguarding lead through your normal route, immediately — and none of it goes into an AI tool, for any purpose, including help with wording the referral.

1.7 · Four decisions before a tool touches a classroom

DecisionWhat good looks like
ApprovalThe tool is on your school's approved list, under a school account, with a written agreement covering where data goes. If it is not on the list, you use it with your own planning only — never with anything about a student, and never with students logged in. A tool used under your personal email puts student data in an account your school cannot audit, cannot delete from, and could not produce if a parent made an access request.
ConsentIf students will use the tool themselves, parental consent is obtained in writing beforehand — naming the tool, the purpose, the data involved, and how to decline without disadvantage. Tracked per student, not per class: one unreturned form is one student who cannot take part, and you need to know which. Check the tool's own minimum age for the year group before the lesson, not after the complaint.
MinimisationThe least data that makes it work. No sign-up where a link will do. No real names in accounts where initials or a number will do. No photographs at all, unless there is a specific reason and a specific consent.
RetentionSomeone knows when it gets deleted and can do it. A class set of accounts nobody deletes in July is a breach waiting for a year to happen in.

1.8 · When it goes wrong

Assume that at some point someone pastes something they should not have. What separates a manageable incident from a serious one is almost entirely what happens in the next hour.

  1. Stop. Do not carry on with the conversation or the tool.
  2. Do not quietly delete and hope. Deleting your own chat destroys the record, removes nothing from the processor's systems, and turns a mistake into a concealment.
  3. Tell the designated safeguarding or data protection lead the same day. Reporting timelines are counted in hours, and they start when the school finds out, not when you decide it is serious.
  4. Write down what went in, which tool, which account, when. Five lines. That is what the school needs to assess it.

Harm — and what you are required to do

Module 1 was about information. This one is about harm. They are routinely treated as the same subject and they are not — a school can have immaculate data practice and still fail a child.

The distinction that organises this module

Data protection asks what happens to information about a child. Safeguarding asks what happens to the child. Everything in Module 1 was the first. Everything here is the second, and only the second carries a duty on you personally that is written into federal law.

2.1 · Reporting is a legal duty, not a school procedure

This is the single most important paragraph in the course, and most AI guidance for teachers does not contain it.

Federal Law No. 3 of 2016 — Wadeema's Law

Article 42 places a duty on any person who knows that a child is exposed to harm to report it to the appropriate authorities, including the police and child protection units.

Article 43 places a specific duty on named professionals — including teachers, healthcare workers and social workers — to report signs of abuse, neglect or mistreatment.

The obligation is yours. It is not delegated to you by your school and it cannot be withdrawn by your school. “I told my line manager and they said to leave it” is not a defence, because the duty was never theirs to discharge on your behalf.

Not legal advice. Article numbers and the scope of the duty are stated so you can find the instrument and ask the right question of the right person. Your school's designated safeguarding lead and its legal counsel determine what applies in your setting. The full text is published by the Ministry of Justice and is linked in the references.

2.4 · AI and wellbeing

The second category the guidance misses. Conversational systems are designed to be agreeable and available, which makes them unusually attractive to a child who is isolated, and unusually poor at the moment that matters.

  • Companion use and dependency. A chatbot that is always patient and never busy can displace the human relationships a struggling child most needs. Worth noticing, not pathologising.
  • Disclosure to a machine. Children tell these systems things they will not tell an adult. Nothing on the other end is required to act, to tell anyone, or to be right.
  • Harmful content. Self-harm, disordered eating and suicide are areas where a general-purpose model's output is unpredictable and where a child in distress is least able to judge it.
  • Displacement of help-seeking. The most serious risk is not a bad answer. It is a child who asked something and now believes they have dealt with it.
What this means for you

If a child tells you they have been discussing something difficult with an AI, that is a disclosure and it goes to the DSL by the normal route. Do not ask to read the conversation, do not ask them to send it to you, and do not put any of it into a tool yourself. And when you design tasks, do not build a reflective or pastoral activity on top of a chatbot — Module 1 made that point about data; it is a stronger point about wellbeing.

What makes a task worth setting

A task is not demanding because of what it is about. A task is demanding because of what a student has to do to finish it. These come apart more often than we would like.

4.1 · Topic is not demand

Two Year 6 classes study the same thing — the causes of the First World War, or the water cycle, or fractions of an amount. One class copies a labelled diagram and answers five recall questions. The other is handed four sources that do not agree and has to decide which to believe and say why. Same topic. Different subject, really, because the thinking is different.

This is the distinction the whole course rests on, and it is worth being blunt about how easily it hides. A task can look rigorous and demand almost nothing. Long tasks, difficult vocabulary, many steps, a whole lesson spent — none of these is the same as a task that requires a student to reason. A worksheet with thirty questions may demand less thinking than one well-built question.

Looks demanding

“Research the causes of the First World War and produce a poster. Include at least five facts, three images and a timeline.”

Is demanding

“Here are four historians who each name a different main cause. They cannot all be right. Which one would you have to prove wrong first, and what evidence would do it?”

The first task is longer, prettier and takes more lesson time. A student can complete it entirely by finding and relocating information. The second can be answered in a paragraph and cannot be completed without reasoning.

4.2 · The question that has become unavoidable

There is a version of this that teachers were able to postpone until about three years ago, and cannot now.

Ask of any task

What is actually being assessed here — and can a machine do that part?

If writing is being assessed, a student submitting generated prose has submitted someone else's writing, and that is plagiarism in the ordinary sense. If ideas are being assessed, generated ideas are not the student's ideas. But run the test the other way and it stops being a policing question and becomes a design one: a task that a model can complete was, in all likelihood, already a task that a student could complete without thinking. The machine did not create the weakness. It exposed it.

Which means the two questions in this module are the same question:

The pedagogy versionCould a capable student finish this correctly without understanding it?
The 2026 versionCould a model finish this correctly without being the student?

Tasks that survive the second usually survive the first. That is the most useful thing AI has done for task design, and it did it by accident.

4.3 · Fluency, and fluency plus

None of this argues against practice. Students need fluency — recall, procedure, spelling, number fact — and they need it automatic, because thinking is expensive and working memory is small. A student still working out 7 × 8 has nothing left over for the problem the multiplication sat inside.

The distinction that matters, drawn from Professor Martin Westwell's work in the South Australian system, is between fluency and fluency plus. Fluency is the practised thing, done accurately and quickly. Fluency plus is the practised thing used in a situation the student has to read first — where knowing the procedure is necessary and not sufficient, because they also have to decide that this is the procedure to use.

The useful test

Could a student complete this correctly without understanding it? If yes — by following the template, copying the structure, applying the one procedure that was obviously going to be the procedure — it is a fluency task. That is fine, as long as you know that is what it is, and as long as it is not the only kind of task the week contains.

4.4 · The four strategies

The rest of this module uses the Transforming Tasks framework — four strategies, each with four named techniques, developed and published by the South Australian Department for Education within its Teaching for Effective Learning (TfEL) framework. It is used here because it is unusually practical: each technique is a change you can make to a task you have already written, in minutes, without redesigning the unit.

From closed to open

A task with one right answer, reached one way, becomes a task with a range — without becoming vague. Openness is not the absence of constraint. The best open tasks are tightly constrained and still admit several good answers.

Different perspectivesStudents respond from more than one viewpoint, not just the one correct reading.
Many solutionsThe task stays open-ended, but carries constraints that stretch rather than narrow.
Many pathwaysSeveral valid routes to a solution, not one prescribed method.
Many entry pointsStudents start wherever they can access it — including working backwards from the outcome.

From tell to ask

The teacher stops supplying the understanding and starts asking for it. The hardest of the four to hold under time pressure, because telling is faster in the moment and slower over a term.

Explore before explainStudents investigate the phenomenon before you name or explain the concept.
Socratic questioningA chain of probing questions draws the thinking out instead of stating it.
Student voiceA genuine opportunity for students to shape the direction of the task or discussion.
Use dialogueStructured back-and-forth between students, not only teacher-to-student telling.

From procedure to problem solving

The most demanding of the four, and it assumes the other three. Students work out what to do rather than being told what to do.

Students identify the problemA scenario is given; students work out what the actual problem to solve is.
Insufficient informationA piece is withheld, so students must notice it is missing and ask for it.
Only some of the stepsPartial working or instructions; students fill the gaps.
Irrelevant informationA distractor students must recognise and set aside.

From information to understanding

Push past recall towards synthesis. The strategy most often skipped, because information tasks are the easiest to set and the easiest to mark.

Many ways of knowingThe same understanding represented in more than one mode — visual, verbal, physical.
Compare and contrastSimilarities and differences between two related ideas, texts or cases.
Make connections, find relationshipsStudents explicitly link this idea to something they already know.
GeneraliseStudents draw the general principle or rule out of specific examples.

4.5 · Three rebuilds, three phases

Before · Year 3 science

“Label the parts of a plant. Write one sentence about what each part does.”

After · insufficient information

“This plant has been in the cupboard for two weeks. Draw what you think it looks like now and label why. You may ask me three questions about the cupboard before you start.”

Techniques used: insufficient information, many ways of knowing. Same content knowledge required. The student now has to work out what they need to know, and the drawing carries the reasoning so the writing demand does not gate the science.

Before · Year 6 maths

“Find 3/4 of 60, 2/5 of 45, 5/8 of 96.”

After · many solutions, irrelevant information

“A shop is offering three deals. Sara has AED 60 and wants the most sweets. One deal is a distractor — it cannot be the best under any spend. Find it, prove it, then say which deal you would take and when you would change your mind.”

Techniques used: irrelevant information, many solutions, many pathways. The fraction work is still there and is now necessary but not sufficient — the student has to decide it is the thing to do.

Before · Year 9 English

“Write a paragraph explaining how the writer creates tension in this extract. Use PEEL.”

Techniques used: compare and contrast, different perspectives. PEEL can still be the scaffold; it is no longer the task. And note what happened to the second question in 3.2 — a model can produce the first paragraph and cannot make the editorial judgement without having made it.

4.7 · Where AI helps at this stage

Most teachers meet AI at the wrong end of this process: they ask for a task. You saw in Module 3 what that produces, and now you know why — the model averages to the most common pattern, and the most common pattern is a fluency task. At the audit stage it is far more useful, for three narrow jobs:

  • Naming the demand. “Describe only what a student must do in their head to finish this. Do not evaluate the task or suggest improvements.” The constraint matters — models default to praise and rewrite.
  • Technique matching. “Which of these four strategies is entirely absent from this task?”, with the framework pasted in.

Model answer

Two things are usually true at once, and they need separating.

The first is real: a class taught for years that every task has one answer the teacher already has will treat an open task as a guessing game about the teacher's mind. The freezing is rational behaviour. It is not evidence the students cannot think; it is evidence they have learned what school rewards. It goes away, but over weeks, not in one lesson, and only if the first open tasks are genuinely safe — no marks attached, wrong answers used rather than corrected, and the teacher visibly not knowing the answer at least once.

The second is a design fault that gets blamed on the students: an open task with no constraints is not open, it is vague. “Write about the water cycle” produces paralysis. “Here are three places the water could have gone; only one is impossible — which, and how would you show it?” is open, bounded, and enterable. 5.1 · The four rungs

SAMR — Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition — was proposed by Dr Ruben Puentedura as a way of describing what a digital tool is doing to a task. Four rungs, usually drawn as a ladder with two halves: the bottom two enhance an existing task, the top two transform it.

SSubstitution

The tool replaces an older tool. The task itself is unchanged — if you removed the technology, you would still have the same task on paper.

In a classroom: the worksheet becomes a PDF. The essay is typed instead of handwritten. The textbook page is on a screen.

Data exposure: essentially none — no account, nothing collected.
AAugmentation

Still the same task, but the tool adds a functional improvement the paper version could not offer.

In a classroom: the quiz marks itself and tells the student why the wrong answer was attractive. The document reads itself aloud. The diagram can be dragged and relabelled.

Data exposure: low, unless the scores are stored against a name.
MModification

The task is redesigned. The technology has changed what students are being asked to do, not just how they do it.

In a classroom: students collect their own data with a sensor and interrogate the graph it draws. Drafts are peer-reviewed in comments and the revision history is the evidence of thinking.

Data exposure: real. Accounts, stored work, student-to-student channels.
RRedefinition

A task that could not have been set at all before. Not a better version of something — a different thing.

In a classroom: a class in Dubai and a class in Nairobi test the same hypothesis on their own local data and have to reconcile two honest sets of results. Students publish to a real audience that answers back.

Data exposure: highest. Audio or video of children, publication, third parties, cross-border transfer.

5.2 · Where the ladder misleads

SAMR is useful and it is also the most over-claimed framework in edtech, so it is worth being straight about what it will and will not bear.

Three honest caveats
  • It is a lens, not a league table. It describes what the technology is doing. It makes no claim about learning, and the research base for “higher rungs produce better outcomes” is thin — the framework has been criticised on exactly this point for being a hierarchy without evidence that the hierarchy matters.
  • Redefinition is not the goal. A fluent, well-designed Substitution task is better than a Redefinition task nobody can finish. The right rung is the one the learning needs, which is usually not the top one.
  • It says nothing at all about thinking. This is the one that matters for this course, and it is the next section.

5.3 · Two ladders, not one

Here is the move that makes SAMR worth 35 minutes. Put it on one axis and the demand question from Module 4 on the other. They are independent — and once you can see that, most edtech disappointment explains itself.

↑ High cognitive demand  ·  → High SAMR

Low tech · high demand

Four contradictory sources on paper and one question: which would you have to prove wrong first? Pencil, no device, enormous thinking. This is a good lesson. It is also the quadrant SAML-driven planning quietly treats as a failure.

High tech · high demand

Your class and another school test the same hypothesis on local data and have to reconcile two honest, conflicting results. The technology is doing something irreplaceable and the reasoning is real. Rare, expensive, worth it when the content justifies it.

Low tech · low demand

The worksheet, typed. Everyone recognises this one as weak, which is why it is the least dangerous cell on the grid.

High tech · low demand

A polished group documentary assembled from copied facts. Green screen, soundtrack, published to the world — Redefinition by any reading, and not one student had to reason about anything. This is the dangerous cell, because it looks like the best lesson in the school.

The rule this gives you

Climb the demand ladder first. Climb the SAMR ladder only as far as the demand requires — and then only as far as the safeguarding load will bear. A task that moves up SAMR without moving up demand has bought you nothing and cost you something.

5.6 · The thing the ladder never shows you

Look back at the data-exposure line under each rung in 4.1. Read them downward and a second pattern appears that the SAMR diagram does not draw:

Say it out loud once

SAMR is also a data-exposure ladder. Every rung you climb, the task collects more about the child.

Substitution collects nothing — a PDF is a PDF. Augmentation starts storing responses. Modification needs accounts, saved work and student-to-student channels. Redefinition, almost by definition, means a real audience, which means publication, which frequently means a child's voice, face, name or work leaving the school.

This is not an argument for staying at the bottom. It is an argument for knowing what you are buying. The question “is this worth it?” has two halves, and most planning asks only the first:

The half everyone asksDoes the technology make the learning better than the paper version?
The half almost nobody asksIs that improvement worth the data this task will now collect about children — and who decided that?

⬛ Safeguarding by design · Module 6

Every rung you climb, you collect more

You have just learned a framework whose entire direction of travel is towards tools that hold more about children. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to make the decision in the open rather than by drift.

  • Pick the lowest rung that delivers the demand. If Augmentation gets the thinking you wanted, Modification is a cost with no return.
  • Price the rung before you commit. Moving from Augmentation to Modification usually means accounts, stored work and a retention plan. That is a conversation with your data lead, not a Sunday-evening decision.
  • Redefinition almost always means publication. A real audience is the point of the rung — so consent, what is shown, and whose face or voice appears are part of the design, not an afterthought at the end of the unit.
  • Never let the rung justify the data. “It had to be Redefinition” is not a lawful basis for processing a child's personal information.

Differentiating without lowering the demand

Module 1 is a gate. Pass the check at the end of it and this opens. Go to Module 1 →

Most differentiation quietly solves the wrong problem. It makes the task reachable by making it smaller. The student who needed the most thinking gets the least, every lesson, for years — and the gap it was meant to close is the thing it widens.

6.1 · One destination, several roads

The rule

Differentiate the route, not the destination. Every student in the room is going to the same understanding. What changes is the scaffold, the entry point, the amount of text they have to decode to get in, the support available on the way, and how they show you they arrived. What does not change is what they have to understand by the end.

Which means the first thing to write down is not the three versions of the worksheet. It is the destination, stated precisely enough that you can tell whether someone reached it. The handbook convention for this is KUD — what students should Know, Understand, and be able to Do.

PartWhat goes in itExample · Year 5, forces
KnowFacts, vocabulary, definitions. Recallable. This is where differentiation is least appropriate — everyone needs the vocabulary.Friction, air resistance, newton, balanced and unbalanced forces.
UnderstandThe transferable idea, written as a full sentence beginning “that…”. This never differs between students.…that an object's motion changes only when the forces on it are unbalanced, whatever the object is.
DoThe skill, observable. This is where the route legitimately varies — a student can show the same reasoning by writing, building, annotating or explaining aloud.Predict and justify what happens to an object when one force is changed.

If you cannot write the U as a sentence, the task is probably an activity rather than a task, and no amount of differentiation will fix that. Write the U first. Every version is then judged against it.

Readiness is not ability

Readiness is where a student is with this specific idea, today — and it moves constantly. Ability is a claim about the student. A readiness group that survives from September to July is not a readiness group; it is a set, drawn quietly, and it will do what sets do. Regroup on the evidence, every unit, and let students see the groups change.

Learning profile is also not learning styles. The claim that matching instruction to a preferred modality improves learning has not held up in research. What holds up is that the content has a best modality — you cannot learn a map from a description — and that students work productively in different social configurations. Vary it for those reasons.

6.3 · You cannot differentiate for readiness you have not measured

Grouping on impression is grouping on last year's impression. Pre-assessment for this purpose does not have to be long — it has to be diagnostic, which means the wrong answers have to tell you something.

  • Two-tier question. A multiple-choice item, then “why did you choose that?” The second tier is the whole value: two students pick the same right answer for entirely different reasons.
  • Agree or disagree provocation. One statement built on the misconception you expect. The split in the room is your grouping.
  • Annotated drawing. Draw what happens and label why. Surfaces reasoning from students whose writing would hide it.
  • I used to think… now I think… Run at the end, read before the next lesson, used to regroup.

Four to six minutes, three days before you teach the thing, is enough. Seven days before is better, because it leaves time for the task you write to be the right one.

6.5 · The tiering trap

You have already seen this once, in the Module 3 worksheet. Here it is stated as a principle, because almost every set of tiered materials in circulation contains it.

Tiered down — the trap

Top: “Evaluate which factor contributed most to the fall of the empire.”
Middle: “Explain two causes of the fall of the empire.”
Support: “Match the cause to the picture. Copy the sentence.”

Tiered for access — same U

All: “Only one of these three causes could have been removed and the empire still falls. Which, and how do you know?”
Support has: the three causes summarised in one line each, a word bank, and a partner to talk to first.
Extension has: a fourth cause that does not fit the pattern.

In the first, the verbs descend — evaluate, explain, match — so the thinking descends with them, and the support group has been given a copying task. In the second, every student does the same reasoning. What differs is how much reading stands between them and the reasoning, whether they talk before they write, and what happens when they finish early.

Three questions that catch it
  1. Is the U identical across all versions? If the sentence beginning “that…” changes, you have tiered the destination.
  2. Do the verbs descend? Evaluate / explain / match is a warning, not a plan.
  3. Could a student in the support version ever produce the evidence the top version asks for? If not on any day this term, the support version is a holding pen.

6.6 · Bias is a differentiation failure before it is an ethics topic

A language model's output follows its training distribution, and that distribution is the published internet: predominantly English, predominantly North American and European, and carrying every assumption those texts carry about who does what. People usually meet this as an abstraction about image searches. In this module it is concrete and it costs students something.

Ask a model to produce support materials and watch what it assumes:

What you ask forWhat the distribution supplies unless you stop it
“A version for students who find this hard”Colouring, matching, cutting and sticking. Calm, pleasant, undemanding — and a judgement about who gets to think, which you just imported.
“An extension for the top group”Debate, independent research, leadership of others. The interesting work, reserved.
A context or character for a word problemDefaults on gender, occupation and family shape that your class may not recognise, and will notice.
Names in examplesA narrow and predictable set. In a Gulf or international classroom this is immediately conspicuous.

The Module 3 output did all four — colouring for the support group, debate for the top group — and it did so because the teacher's own prompt described children by origin and reading age, which invited it. That is the two failures meeting: unsafe input producing biased output.

The counters, in the brief
  • Never describe the students. Describe the task and the destination, and let the routes differ by scaffold rather than by who you think will take them.
  • Forbid the pattern explicitly: “no colouring, matching, cutting or copying in any version.”
  • Ask for the reasoning to be identical and say so: “all three versions must require the same justification.”
  • Name your own context: “students in a Gulf international school; use names and settings that reflect that, or none at all.”

6.7 · Separating language load from cognitive load

In a school with EAL learners — which in this region is most schools — the commonest way demand gets lowered is by accident. The task is hard to read, so it is replaced by a task that is easy to think about. Those are different dials and they need turning separately.

Turn this downKeep this up
Sentence length and clause density in the stemThe number of things that must be held in mind at once
Low-frequency words that are not the subject vocabularyThe subject vocabulary itself — everyone needs it, taught explicitly
Amount of text to decode before the thinking startsThe requirement to justify, compare or decide
Writing required to show the reasoningThe reasoning

Practically: shorten the stem, pre-teach the three subject words, allow the answer as a labelled diagram or thirty seconds of talk, and leave the question alone. A student who can explain it aloud and not write it has a writing problem, and you have just found it — which is more useful than hiding it inside a simplified task.

6.8 · Getting differentiated materials out of AI

This is where AI earns its place, and the reason is unglamorous: the labour of producing three genuine versions is what stops most teachers doing it. A model produces those versions in under a minute. It also produces the tiering trap by default, every time, unless you forbid it.

⬛ Before you paste — this is the module where people slip

  • Nothing about the students goes in. Not names, not reading ages, not “one has a processing difficulty”, not a class list, not a work sample.
  • The spread goes in as numbers and generalities: “28, mixed, roughly a third below age-related expectations in reading”. That is all a model has ever needed to produce these materials.
  • If you find yourself about to explain why a particular child needs the support version, stop. That sentence is the one that causes the incident.

Prompt pattern · three access points, one destination

Here is a task I set and the single understanding all students must reach: TASK: [paste] UNDERSTANDING (U): that [one sentence — the destination] CLASS: [numbers and generalities only — no individuals] Produce three versions. All three must require the same reasoning and evidence the same U. You may vary ONLY: · how much text must be read before the thinking begins · whether a worked example, sentence stem or word bank is supplied · whether students talk before writing · the form the answer may take You may NOT vary the cognitive verb, remove the justification, or replace reasoning with recall, matching, copying or colouring. Do not ask me for information about individual students; I will not provide it and you do not need it. For each version, state the U it evidences. If any version evidences a different U, say so and fix it before showing me.

Then — and this is the part that is skipped — you check what comes back. Three checks, thirty seconds each:

  • The destination check. Read the support version alone. Could a student who completed it correctly state the U? If not, reject it.
  • The floor check. Can your least confident student start it in the first two minutes without you? If not, the entry point is missing, not the difficulty.
  • The ceiling check. Can your most confident student finish it in five minutes with nothing further to think about? Then the extension is a quantity of work rather than a depth of thought.

⬛ Safeguarding by design · Module 7

Differentiation is where the data leaks

Of everything in this course, this is the module whose daily practice most often puts a child's information somewhere it should not be — because to differentiate you first have to know something about the learner, and that knowledge is exactly what must not travel.

  • Describe routes, never children. “A version with a shorter stem and a word bank” gets you the same materials as “a version for Ahmed, who has dyslexia” — and only one of those is a disclosure.
  • The standing ability list is the single most dangerous document in a school. Named children, special educational needs, reading ages, in one file, copied forward year on year, and the first thing anyone reaches for when a model asks about the class. If a record of grouping must exist, it belongs to the unit and expires with it.
  • The support route collects more. Worked examples, hint usage, talk-first prompts, longer response times — the scaffolded version naturally generates more data about the students who need it most. If your build logs anything, check it is not quietly logging who needed help.
  • Pre-assessment is personal data the moment it has a name on it. Two-tier questions and annotated drawings are superb diagnostics and are student work. They do not get photographed into a tool.
  • Banding is visible to children. A route labelled by ability in front of the class is a disclosure to thirty peers, whatever the data policy says. Name the routes by what they offer, not by who they are for.

1

Standalone revision

One file. No accounts, no server, no state that outlives the tab.

Load · none

The workhorse, and the one most teachers skip straight past on the way to something more impressive. It is a document that checks itself. A student opens it, works through, and is told why they were wrong — which is the one thing a printed revision sheet can never do.

Choose it when the content is settled, the misconceptions are known, and what students need is another honest encounter with the material rather than a collaboration.

The spine

01
Decide the file is the whole thing.No build step, no dependencies, no login. It must open from an email attachment on a four-year-old school laptop with the wifi off.
02
Put the content in a data structure, not in the markup.An array of sections, each with a heading, a body and its checks. You will rewrite the content ten times and the layout once — so make the content the cheap part to change.
03
Write the wrong answers before the right one.Each distractor is a misconception you have actually seen in a book. The feedback for it is a question that sends the student back to the evidence, never a correction.
04
Give every section a way out and a way up.A worked example for whoever cannot start, and one open question at the end for whoever finished in four minutes.
05
Make it print.A print stylesheet that opens every collapsed panel. Half your class will want it on paper, and the ones who want it on paper are often the ones who need it most.
06
Store nothing.Not even progress. If a student closes the tab they start again, and for a twenty-minute revision sheet that is the correct trade.

2

Revision with a lab and a certificate

Adds a practice space that does not count, and a record at the end that does.

Load · very low

Architecture 1 plus two things: a lab — somewhere to attempt, fail and reattempt with no score attached — and an output the learner keeps. The lab is the pedagogy. The certificate is the reason they finish the lab.

Choose it when the skill needs repetition under feedback, or when somebody other than the student needs evidence that it happened.

The spine

01
Separate the lab from the assessment, visibly.Students behave differently when attempts are free. Say so on the screen: nothing here is recorded. Then have one assessment that is.
02
Model the course as data.Modules → lessons → checks, with a type on each lesson (reading, activity, case, quiz). Everything else — the progress bar, the nav, the completion logic — reads from that one structure.
03
Let the lab be retried without limit, and show the reasoning after the second attempt.First attempt: a question back. Second: the worked reasoning. Unlimited attempts with no explanation trains guessing.

05

3

Tiered differentiated outcomes

One destination. Several bands. The U written once, at the top, shared by all of them.

Load · low, with a catch

Module 6 argued the principle. This is the structure that enforces it — because the reason tiering fails in practice is that the three versions live in three separate documents, and nothing stops them drifting apart.

Choose it when readiness varies widely and the concept is non-negotiable.

The spine

01
The U is a single value in the data, not a sentence repeated three times.One string, at the top, referenced by every band. If it can only be written once, it cannot drift — the architecture does what the willpower was failing to do.
02
Band is a property of an item, not a separate document.Each task object carries band: 'Support' | 'Core' | 'Push'. Three worksheets become one structure with a filter, and a side-by-side view becomes possible — which is how you catch a descending verb.
03
Vary only the four permitted dials.Reading load before the thinking starts; whether a worked example, stem or word bank is supplied; whether talk comes before writing; the form the answer may take. Build the editor so those are the only fields that differ, and the trap becomes hard to fall into.
04
Let students choose their band, with a nudge.Self-selection beats assignment on engagement and is usually accurate within one band. Show all three. Let anyone move up mid-task — the move upward is the most useful thing the task will tell you.
05
Every band ends at the same rubric,with a next step written at every level — including at the top. “Excelling” with no next step teaches a student that they have finished learning.
06
Build the side-by-side check into the teacher view.Three bands in three columns with the U above all of them. Read the verbs down the row. If they descend, you can see it in one glance, which is the only reliable way anyone catches it.

Exemplars: Writers Workshop — Support & Push — bands, rubric and a next step at every level. The Writer's Expedition — Support/Core/Push models and drills per power, with the band carried on the item rather than the document.

4

Finding Out collaborative

Built for the inquiry stage where students gather, judge and synthesise — and where copy-paste is the default failure.

Load · medium

The Finding Out stage of an inquiry is the one most likely to collapse into googling and relocating. The architecture exists to make that collapse structurally difficult: students cannot proceed until they have generated their own questions, and cannot submit what they have not put in their own words.

Choose it when a group is researching a shared question and you want the research itself to be the thinking, not the preamble to it.

The spine

01
Roles are jobs with outputs, not labels.“Data Officer” who must read every reading aloud and say whether it is inside the band. “Scribe” who types the verdict in full sentences. If a role can be held by someone doing nothing, it is a badge, not a role — and roles rotate, so nobody is the scribe for a term.
02
Question-building is a gate before research opens.Students write the questions they intend to answer, and the task does not unlock the research stage until they have. A group that researches before it has questions is a group that will find whatever it finds and call that the answer.
03
Capture evidence in a structure that refuses a paste.Short fields, a word limit, a source line that must be filled, and a summary step that explicitly asks for their own words. You will not stop determined copying; you will stop lazy copying, which is almost all of it.
04
Put a synthesis gate before the product.“Are you really done researching?” — a short honest self-check that sends most groups back once. This is the single highest-value screen in the architecture.
05
Group product, individual accountability.The group makes one artefact. Each student separately answers something only a participant could answer — what your group decided and what argued against it.
06
Shared state by room code, never by name.A group joins with a code and a role. No logins, no email, no real names in the shared record — a role and a group number carry everything the task needs.

Exemplar: Finding Out: Body Systems Expedition — Collaborative — base camp, roles, question building, research time, the “are you really done?” gate, and a no-copy-paste summary step before the slides.

5

Multi-user interdependent outcomes

An answer that is impossible alone, because the information is split and no part is sufficient.

Load · high

The most demanding architecture and the easiest to fake. Most “collaborative” tasks are parallel tasks at one table. This one is not, because the design makes individual completion impossible — the interdependence is structural rather than requested.

Choose it when there is a genuine trade-off or disagreement worth having, and the content divides honestly.

The spine

01
Design the asymmetry first, before anything else.Who holds what, and why no one holding can answer alone. If you cannot state that in one sentence, you do not have this architecture yet — you have group work.
02
Make the local optimum the wrong answer.The best site on flood risk must not be the best site overall. Each student's own data should argue for a different conclusion, so agreement requires trading rather than voting.
03
Build a combination gate.A final step that only opens on inputs from more than one role. This is the escape-room code logic from Module 4, applied across people instead of across stages.
04
Decide what the shared state is allowed to hold.Group number, role, the contributed data, a timestamp. Not names, not free text that could identify, not anything a child types about themselves. Write that list before you write the schema, because retrofitting it is how fields survive that should not.
05
Individual accountability at the end, always.One question each, answerable only by someone who took part. Without it a group mark rewards the one student who did it and teaches the other three what group work is for.
06
The teacher board is aggregate.Where groups are up to, what is stuck, which misconception is most common. Never a live ranking with children's names on a projector — that is a wellbeing decision dressed as a motivation feature.

Exemplars: The Evidence Bureau — sign-in, open cases, drills, spotlight method, class board. Body Balance Detectives — five stations, five rotating roles, timed rotation, aggregate debrief. Progress Record Book — the teacher-facing aggregate end of the same shape.

⬛ Safeguarding by design · Module 7

The architecture is the safeguarding decision

You have just been taught five shapes whose data footprint rises from nothing to considerable. Which one you reach for is, before it is anything else, a decision about what this task will now hold about children.

  • Start at 1 and climb only when the learning forces you. Most of what gets built as Architecture 4 would have worked as Architecture 2 with a sharper question.
  • Write the “what this may hold” list before the schema. In Architecture 5 especially: group number, role, contribution, timestamp — and nothing a child types about themselves. A field added later is a field nobody decided on.
  • Never put a named ranking on a projector. Every one of these architectures can produce one, and none of them needs one.
  • Consent and approval belong to the architecture, not the lesson. If you build at 4 or 5, you have committed your school to accounts, storage and a retention plan. That is a conversation to have before the build, not before the lesson.
  • When you brief a model, name the architecture and forbid the rest. Left to itself it will add a login and a leaderboard, because that is what its training data does.
